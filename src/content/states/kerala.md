## Overview

Kerala is a narrow state on India's south-west coast, between the Arabian Sea and the Western Ghats. It borders Karnataka to the north and Tamil Nadu to the east, and the small enclave of Mahé, part of the union territory of Puducherry, lies within it. The state had about 33 million people at the 2011 Census and covers 38,852 square kilometres.

The capital is Thiruvananthapuram, and the Kochi metropolitan area is the largest urban region. Kerala had the highest literacy rate (94.0%) and the highest sex ratio (1,084 women per 1,000 men) of any state at the 2011 Census. It is one of India's best-known travel destinations, with backwaters, beaches, hill stations and Ayurvedic treatments.

The state was formed on 1 November 1956 by uniting the Malayalam-speaking regions of the south-west. Malayalam and English are its official languages.

## History

Kerala has traded spices with the wider world for thousands of years. Its first major kingdom was that of the Cheras, and in the last centuries BCE Roman ships came to the port of Muziris for black pepper. Early communities of Christians, Jews and Muslims took root along the coast: the Cheraman Juma Masjid is regarded as India's first mosque, and the Paradesi Synagogue in Kochi was built in 1568.

In 1498 the Portuguese navigator Vasco da Gama landed near Kozhikode (Calicut), then ruled by the Zamorin. The Portuguese, then the Dutch and finally the British competed for the spice trade. In the 18th century, Marthanda Varma of Travancore defeated the Dutch at the Battle of Colachel (1741) and made Travancore the dominant power in the south. Travancore and Kochi later became princely states under British paramountcy, while Malabar in the north was governed directly as part of the Madras Presidency.

After independence, Travancore and Kochi merged in 1949 to form Travancore-Cochin. On 1 November 1956, Malabar and the Kasaragod area were added and some southern areas transferred to Madras State, creating Kerala. It has had 14 districts since 1984.

## Geography and climate

Kerala has three broad bands: the low-lying **coast**, with its lagoons and backwaters; the **midlands** of rolling hills and farmland; and the **highlands** of the Western Ghats. Anamudi, in Idukki district, is the highest peak in South India at 2,695 m.

The state has 44 rivers, the longest of which is the Periyar. Vembanad, between Alappuzha and Kochi, is India's longest lake and the heart of the backwaters. Four of Kerala's wetlands, including Vembanad-Kol and Ashtamudi, are Ramsar sites.

Kerala is one of the wettest parts of India. About 65% of its rain falls in the south-west monsoon (June to August), and most of the rest in the north-east monsoon (September to December).

## Economy

According to Invest India, Kerala's economy was estimated at ₹6.85 lakh crore in 2024–25, with a per-capita income of ₹1,90,149, well above the national average. Services dominate, including tourism, health care, Ayurveda, trade and information technology.

Remittances from Keralites working abroad, especially in the Gulf countries, have long been a major source of income and make up a significant share of the state's economy. Kerala is also a leading producer of natural rubber and black pepper, and coconut, tea, coffee, cashew and other spices are important crops. Fishing supports a large coastal population.

## Culture, art and festivals

Kerala's classical and folk arts are among the most distinctive in India. They include **Kathakali**, the elaborately costumed dance-drama, **Mohiniyattam**, a graceful solo dance, the ritual performance of **Theyyam** in the north, and the martial art **Kalaripayattu**.

**Onam**, the harvest festival, is celebrated by people of all communities with flower carpets, feasts and boat races. Vishu, Thrissur Pooram, Christmas and Eid are also major occasions. Kerala has a strong tradition of public reading and a lively press, with newspapers published in many languages.

## Food

Kerala food is built on rice, coconut, seafood and spices. Breakfasts include **appam**, **puttu** and idiyappam. The **sadya**, a vegetarian feast served on a banana leaf, is central to festivals and weddings. Coastal and backwater cooking features fish and seafood such as **karimeen** (pearl-spot fish), and the north is known for **Malabar biryani**.

## Tourist spots

Most visitors combine a few regions. Kochi offers **Fort Kochi** and Mattancherry, the **backwaters** of Alappuzha and Kumarakom are close by, and **Munnar** and **Periyar** lie in the hills. The south has Thiruvananthapuram, the **Padmanabhaswamy Temple** and the cliffs of **Varkala**, while the north has **Bekal Fort**, Theyyam performances and the hills of Wayanad.

## Education and universities

Kerala has a strong public education system. Its universities include the University of Kerala and Cochin University of Science and Technology (CUSAT), and national institutes include NIT Calicut, IIM Kozhikode and IIT Palakkad.

## Getting there (air, rail, road)

**By air:** Kerala has four international airports: Kochi (Cochin), Thiruvananthapuram, Kozhikode (Calicut) and Kannur. Between them they connect Kerala with major Indian cities and the Gulf.

**By rail:** The main railway line runs the length of the state, linking Thiruvananthapuram, Kollam, Kochi, Thrissur, Kozhikode and Kannur with Chennai, Bengaluru, Mumbai (via the Konkan Railway) and Delhi.

**By road:** National Highway 66 runs along the coast, and roads cross the Western Ghats to Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. Interstate buses are frequent.

## Quick tips for visitors

- **Book houseboats carefully.** Choose licensed operators, and agree on route, meals and timings in advance.
- **Respect temple rules.** Some temples, including Padmanabhaswamy, restrict entry and have strict dress codes. Check before you go.
- **Pack for rain.** Even outside the main monsoon, short heavy showers are common, especially in the hills.
- **Try a homestay.** Family-run stays in plantations and backwater villages are a good way to experience local food and life.
